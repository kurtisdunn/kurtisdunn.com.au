# Psychology Layer — kurtisdunn.com.au

## Overview

This document maps psychological principles to specific locations across the site copy and page designs. Each annotation references the page and section from `copy-all-pages.md` and explains which trigger to apply, how, and why.

---

## 1. RECIPROCITY — The Audit as a Gift

**Principle:** People feel obligated to return favours. Give value before asking for anything.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Homepage → Hero** | Frame the audit as genuinely free and valuable — "I'll show you exactly where you're losing time" — not a disguised sales call |
| **Book Audit page → What You'll Get** | List four specific deliverables the visitor walks away with, even if they never hire Kurtis. This makes the audit feel like a gift, not a funnel step |
| **Blog → every post** | End with a useful takeaway or template the reader can use immediately, before any CTA. Give, then ask |
| **ROI Calculator** | Free interactive tool with no gating. Results delivered instantly. Email capture is optional ("Want me to send you a copy?") |
| **Lead magnets** | "50 Tasks to Automate" checklist and Industry Starter Packs are ungated previews of Kurtis's expertise. Generosity builds obligation |

**Implementation notes:**
- Never undermine the gift by revealing it was strategic. The audit genuinely must be valuable standalone.
- The follow-up email after the audit should reference specific things discussed — not a generic pitch. This reinforces that the gift was personal.

## 2. LOSS AVERSION — Frame the Cost of Inaction

**Principle:** Losses feel roughly 2x more painful than equivalent gains. People work harder to avoid losing than to gain.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Homepage → Problem section** | "Every hour spent on repetitive admin is an hour not spent on clients, growth, or getting home at a reasonable time." Frame current state as ongoing loss |
| **Homepage → Solution section** | "The average business I work with saves 10–20 hours a week. That's a part-time employee's worth of output — without the salary, the super, or the HR headache." Lost time = lost money |
| **Industry pages → Problem sections** | Each industry page should quantify the loss: "Your team is spending 15 hours a week on tasks that add zero value to your clients" |
| **Book Audit page → Final CTA** | "15 minutes could save you 15 hours a week" — the asymmetry highlights what they're losing by NOT booking |
| **Accounting page → Problem** | "Your capacity is capped not by talent, but by admin." Frames current state as a ceiling they're stuck under |
| **Exit popup** | "Still doing [task] manually? That's costing you [X] hours this month alone." Loss framing on exit |

**Language patterns to use:**
- "You're currently losing..." (not "You could gain...")
- "Every week you wait costs..." (not "Every week you could save...")
- "Don't let another month of [pain] go by"

**Language patterns to avoid:**
- Don't fabricate urgency. The loss is real (wasted time) — no need to manufacture pressure.

## 3. SOCIAL PROOF — Numbers, Names, and Specifics

**Principle:** People follow what others are doing. Popularity signals quality and safety.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Homepage → Social proof bar** | "150+ workflows automated · 50+ Australian SMBs · 10–20 hours saved per client, per week · 4.9/5 rating." Specific numbers, not round claims |
| **Homepage → Testimonials** | Named clients with title, company, and city. Full quotes describing specific outcomes, not generic praise |
| **Book Audit page → Social proof** | Testimonials specifically about the audit experience — "Best 15 minutes I've spent on the business this year" |
| **Industry pages → Case study teasers** | Industry-specific social proof. A tradie reading the trades page should see a tradie's testimonial, not a lawyer's |
| **All pages → Footer** | "Trusted by 50+ Australian businesses" with a small selection of client logos (anonymised industry icons if full logos unavailable) |
| **Blog posts** | "Join 500+ Australian business owners getting automation tips" (email signup). Number validates the newsletter |

**Implementation rules:**
- Always use specific numbers over round ones (147 > "hundreds")
- Always name the person, their role, and their city
- Rotate testimonials by page relevance — match industry to visitor intent
- Add new testimonials quarterly as client base grows

## 4. AUTHORITY — Founder Expertise as Trust Signal

**Principle:** People defer to experts. Credentials and demonstrated expertise create trust.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **About page → Story** | Kurtis's career history, certifications, and technical depth. Not bragging — just evidence of capability |
| **All pages → Header** | "Kurtis Dunn" as a named person, not a faceless brand. Personal authority > corporate authority for solo operators |
| **Blog → Author bio** | Every post includes author photo, name, one-line credential, and link to About page |
| **Homepage → Trust section** | "Founder-led, start to finish" — frames the solo model as a feature (direct access to the expert), not a limitation |
| **Case studies** | Detailed methodology descriptions show Kurtis knows what he's doing. "Here's exactly how I approached this" |
| **Schema markup** | `Person` schema with credentials. AI systems and search engines recognise authority signals in structured data |

**Specific authority signals to add:**
- "Featured in" bar if Kurtis gets media mentions (even podcasts or guest posts)
- Years of experience stated explicitly
- Number of automations built (e.g., "200+ automations across 50+ businesses")
- Tool certifications if available (Zapier Expert, Make Partner, etc.)

## 5. COMMITMENT & CONSISTENCY — Micro-Yeses to Macro-Conversion

**Principle:** Once people commit to something small, they want to stay consistent with that commitment. Small steps lead to big ones.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Homepage → Hero assessment form** | The quick-assessment form (name, email, company, challenge) is a micro-commitment. Filling it in primes them for the audit |
| **ROI Calculator** | Entering business data is a commitment act. By the time they see the result, they've invested effort and are more likely to book the audit to discuss it |
| **Blog → Email signup** | Subscribing is a small yes. Subscribers are warmer leads than cold traffic |
| **Book Audit page → Form** | Keep the form short (name, email, business, one question). Each field answered is a micro-commitment towards booking |
| **Audit call itself** | During the call, ask "Does this make sense so far?" and "Would you like me to look at [specific process]?" Each "yes" builds consistency towards the proposal |
| **Post-audit email sequence** | Email 1: "Here's a summary of what we discussed" (they confirm by opening). Email 2: "Here's what the next step looks like" (they consider). Email 3: Proposal. Each step is a small escalation |

**Micro-yes ladder (full journey):**
1. Read a blog post (passive engagement)
2. Download a lead magnet (active engagement, email captured)
3. Use the ROI calculator (effort invested)
4. Book the free audit (commitment escalation)
5. Attend the audit call (time invested)
6. Receive the proposal (considering the investment)
7. Sign the proposal (conversion)

## 6. SCARCITY & URGENCY — Used Sparingly and Honestly

**Principle:** Limited availability increases perceived value. But fake scarcity destroys trust.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement | Honest? |
|-------------------|-----------------|---------|
| **Book Audit page → CTA** | "Available this week. Morning and afternoon slots." This is genuine — Kurtis has limited calendar availability | Yes — calendar-based |
| **Homepage → Final CTA** | "Takes 30 seconds to book. Available this week." Soft urgency without pressure | Yes |
| **Popup → Exit intent** | "I take on 3–4 new projects per month" — frames capacity honestly | Yes — solo operator |
| **Pricing discussions** | "Current project queue is [X] weeks" — natural delivery timeline | Yes |

**What NOT to do:**
- No fake countdown timers
- No "Only 2 spots left" unless genuinely true
- No "Price goes up tomorrow" unless there's a real price change
- No manufactured urgency. Kurtis's genuine constraint (solo operator, limited capacity) IS the scarcity signal

## 7. ANCHORING — Set Price Expectations Before Quoting

**Principle:** The first number people see heavily influences how they judge subsequent numbers.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Homepage → FAQ → "How much does automation cost?"** | "Most projects run between $2,000 and $15,000 depending on complexity." The high anchor ($15,000) makes a $5,000 quote feel reasonable |
| **Homepage → Solution section** | "That's a part-time employee's worth of output — without the salary, the super, or the HR headache." Anchors against hiring cost ($30K–$50K/year) |
| **ROI Calculator** | Shows "Cost of doing nothing: $X per year in wasted hours" before showing automation investment. The waste figure anchors high |
| **Industry pages → Results** | "10–15 hours saved per week" → at $50/hour, that's $750/week → $39,000/year. The value anchor dwarfs the project cost |
| **Case studies** | Lead with the outcome value ("$45,000 in annual savings") before mentioning the project cost ("$8,000 investment") |

**Anchoring hierarchy for the site:**
1. First anchor: Cost of the problem (time, money, errors)
2. Second anchor: Cost of hiring (salary + super + overhead)
3. Third anchor: Project price range (feels small by comparison)
4. Final: Specific quote (positioned as obvious ROI)

## 8. CONTRAST EFFECT — Before/After Framing

**Principle:** Things seem different depending on what they're compared to. Clear contrasts make improvements vivid.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Homepage → Problem section** | Paint a vivid "before" picture (the painful Monday-to-Friday admin cycle). The solution section then creates stark contrast |
| **Industry pages → Solution sections** | Each automation described as "Lead comes in → [automatic steps] → done." The contrast with the manual version (described above) is obvious |
| **Case studies** | Explicit before/after format. "Before: 3 hours per week on invoicing. After: 5 minutes." Use specific numbers, not vague improvements |
| **Testimonials** | Seek quotes that contain natural contrast: "We were doing X. Now we do Y." |
| **Services page → Process** | "From messy to automatic" (Workflow Design subheadline). Contrast is built into the positioning |

## 9. PRATFALL EFFECT — Honest Limitations Build Trust

**Principle:** Showing a small flaw makes competent people more likeable and trustworthy.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **About page → How I Work** | "I work alone, on purpose." Acknowledges the limitation (no team) but reframes it as a deliberate strength |
| **Homepage → FAQ → "Will automation replace my staff?"** | "No." Honest about what automation can't do. This builds more trust than overclaiming |
| **Book Audit page → Objection handling** | "If your business isn't ready for automation, or if the ROI isn't there, I'll tell you." Willingness to turn down business = trustworthy |
| **About page → Values** | "I don't build complex systems to justify a bigger invoice." Implicitly admits the industry sometimes does this. Honesty as differentiation |
| **Homepage → Trust section** | "No lock-in, no proprietary nonsense." Acknowledges industry practices that erode trust, positions Kurtis as different |

## 10. GOAL-GRADIENT EFFECT — Progress Drives Completion

**Principle:** People accelerate effort as they approach a goal. Showing progress motivates action.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Assessment form (/assessment)** | Multi-step form with progress bar. "Step 2 of 4" creates momentum. Users who see they're 50% done are more likely to finish |
| **Book Audit form** | If multi-step, show "Almost there — just one more question" on the final step |
| **Email sequence (post-audit)** | "Here's where we are: ✅ Audit complete ✅ Opportunities identified → Next: Your proposal." Progress visualisation in emails |
| **Onboarding (post-sale)** | "Your automation project: Week 1 of 4 complete." Status updates maintain momentum |

---

## 11. ENDOWMENT EFFECT — Make Them Feel They Already Own the Result

**Principle:** People value things more once they feel they own them.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Book Audit page** | "Your free audit" — possessive language. It's already theirs to claim |
| **ROI Calculator** | "Your estimated savings: $X per year." Once they see their personalised number, they feel ownership of that outcome |
| **Post-audit email** | "Your automation roadmap" — the plan is theirs. Walking away from it means losing something they now own |
| **Industry pages** | "Your quoting process, automated" — possessive framing throughout |

---

## 12. STATUS-QUO BIAS — Reduce the Pain of Change

**Principle:** People prefer the current state, even when change is clearly better. Switching feels risky.

| Where It Applies | How to Implement |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Homepage → Trust section** | "No lock-in, no proprietary nonsense" — reduces the perceived risk of change |
| **Industry pages → FAQ** | "Do I need to change my current tools? Usually not." Reassures that automation adds to their current setup rather than replacing it |
| **Services page → Process** | Step-by-step process removes ambiguity about what happens. Known paths feel safer than unknown ones |
| **Homepage → FAQ → "What if I'm not technical?"** | Addresses the fear that change requires new skills. "Your team keeps using the tools they already know" |
| **All CTAs** | "Free. No obligation. No commitment." Every CTA should feel reversible |
